# 2D Educational Illustration

Draw each subject in the visual language of an older printed educational handbook: straightforward cartoon figures, modest linework and a small set of solid colors. The screenplay supplies the people, objects, setting, actions, text and sequence; this skill supplies only the illustration style.

## When reference pages are supplied

- When redrawing a specific page, preserve its panel count and layout, camera angle, relative scale, character poses, props, arrows and direction of action. Compare every finished page side by side with its corresponding source before accepting it. When a page is supplied only as a style reference for a new subject, borrow its drawing language without copying its layout, props or text.
- Use an explicitly approved redraw as the style anchor for later pages: match its contour weight, flat fills, facial anatomy and limited shading while preserving each new page's distinct composition. Do not copy the approved page's subject or rearrange the new page into a generic scene.
- Check small instructional objects against the source: switch levers and finger direction, alarm bell form, extinguisher hose/nozzle, water bucket, escape hatch, door and elevator controls. These details carry the meaning of the instruction.
- Place exact Vietnamese captions, numbers and narration in editable text or audio layers. Do not rely on generated lettering inside a bitmap. For a request for static pages, keep the illustration still; a timeline may switch pages and play narration without animating the drawing.
- In a narrated preview, reveal the illustrated panels of a multi-scene page one at a time in the screenplay's order. Show the full page only as a closing recap. If a page contains one continuous scene, keep that entire scene visible throughout its phase. A phase without audio can still advance between its still panels.

## Line and shape

- Use thin to medium, slightly uneven near-black contours around characters and props. Keep silhouette lines only modestly stronger than facial details or folds; avoid the heavy outline of a modern sticker or comic.
- Build forms from simple, legible silhouettes and very few interior marks. Draw practical objects with only the parts needed to recognize their use; avoid polished mechanical detail.
- Draw people with credible adult proportions, readable gestures and small, restrained facial features. Keep recurring characters visually consistent across poses when the screenplay uses them.
- Separate overlapping forms with contour lines and clear flat color changes. Keep small objects recognizable at viewing size.

## Color and composition

- Use flat, opaque fills. Favor off-white or pale lavender paper, near-black ink, muted plum, ochre, warm yellow skin and restrained coral accents. Assign colors by visual role in each new subject rather than reusing a previous scene's clothing or object colors.
- Give each illustration a clear focal point and generous open space. A single ground line or plain color field often supplies enough context; add surroundings only when they explain the action.
- Avoid gradients, airbrushed glow, glossy 3D lighting, shadows, dense texture, decorative noise and excessive background detail. A slight imperfection in printed edges is acceptable, but do not add artificial grain as decoration.
- If words or numbers are needed, set them as editable text so their spelling remains accurate. Their content and placement come from the screenplay.

Use `style.json` for the palette and compact rendering cues. Adapt the composition to the current screenplay; no example subject or layout is required.
